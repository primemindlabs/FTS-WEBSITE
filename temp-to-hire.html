Temp to Hire: Is It Right for Your Business?

Fortis Editorial Team
February 24, 2025
5 min read

The temp-to-hire model is one of the most practical hiring tools available to small and mid-size businesses, and one of the most underused. The idea is simple: bring someone on through a staffing agency for a defined period, evaluate them on the actual job, and then make a permanent offer if the fit is right. It sounds straightforward, but a lot of companies get the execution wrong, and end up either not getting value from it or using it as a way to avoid committing to anyone, which creates its own problems. Done right, it dramatically reduces your risk on every hire.

What Temp-to-Hire Actually Means

In a temp-to-hire arrangement, a staffing agency places a worker with a client company on a temporary basis, with the understanding that the placement may convert to a permanent position after a qualifying period. During the temp period, the worker remains an employee of the staffing agency, the agency handles payroll, taxes, and workers' compensation. If and when the conversion happens, the worker becomes a direct employee of the client.

The conversion typically involves a placement fee that the company pays to the agency. This fee varies, usually a percentage of the annual salary, and should be disclosed and agreed upon upfront. The cost of the fee is almost always less than the cost of a bad permanent hire, which is why the model makes economic sense even before you account for the reduced recruitment burden.

The Advantages Most Employers Don't Fully Account For

The obvious advantage is risk reduction: you see the person work before you commit. But there are less obvious advantages that compound over time. Workers who convert from temp-to-hire have typically already been through your onboarding, learned your systems, built relationships with your team, and demonstrated their performance under real conditions. The learning curve on day one of permanent employment is nearly zero compared to a cold hire starting fresh.

There's also a self-selection effect. Workers who accept a temp-to-hire arrangement knowing there's a path to permanence are often more motivated during the temp period than workers who see temporary work as a fallback. They're auditioning. That shifts the dynamic in ways that are visible from the first week.

For the company, it also extends your evaluation window beyond what any interview process can achieve. You see performance under different conditions, a busy week, a slow period, a conflict situation, a learning curve on a new task. The data you have about a candidate after six weeks of real work is categorically richer than anything you can learn in three rounds of interviews.

How to Structure the Temp Period So It Works

The temp period is only valuable if you use it as a genuine evaluation, not just a formality. A few things that make the difference:

  • Set clear expectations from the start. Tell the worker what you're evaluating, what success looks like, and what the path to a permanent offer is. Workers who know what they're working toward perform better and feel more respected.
  • Evaluate actively, not passively. Check in with supervisors every two to three weeks. Don't wait until the end of the temp period to find out there were concerns you could have addressed, or to discover someone exceptional you could have retained sooner.
  • Give feedback early. If something isn't working, say so while there's still time to correct it. A temp period that ends in a no-hire decision after six weeks, with no feedback offered along the way, wastes everyone's time and leaves a bad impression of your company on someone who may talk to others in the market.

Common Mistakes That Make Temp-to-Hire Feel Messy

The companies that have poor experiences with temp-to-hire usually make one of three mistakes. First, they use it as a way to delay making any decision, letting temp periods extend indefinitely without a clear conversion timeline. This creates uncertainty for the worker and often leads to losing good people who accept permanent offers elsewhere.

Second, they don't treat the temp worker like a potential permanent employee. Workers who are managed poorly during the temp period, excluded from team communication, or treated as clearly temporary often disengage, and the performance you evaluate isn't representative of what they'd do with real investment from the company.

Third, they skip the debrief with the staffing agency. If a temp-to-hire placement doesn't convert, that feedback is valuable for the agency's ability to find a better match on the next attempt. Agencies that don't get feedback can't improve.

Is Temp-to-Hire Right for Your Role?

Temp-to-hire works best for roles where performance and culture fit are hard to evaluate in a traditional interview process, where the stakes of a bad permanent hire are high, and where your timeline allows for a qualifying period before you need someone fully committed. Administrative, operations, and office professional roles are ideal. So are roles with specific technical requirements that can only be properly evaluated on the job.

It works less well for highly specialized roles that require intensive onboarding before any real performance is visible, or for roles where you need someone fully committed from day one for culture or confidentiality reasons. For those situations, a direct hire placement with a strong guarantee structure may be a better fit.

The honest answer is that for most small and mid-size Atlanta businesses hiring administrative or operational staff, temp-to-hire should be the default, not the exception. The risk reduction is real, the costs are known, and the outcomes consistently outperform cold hires made from a resume pile. Talk to us about whether it makes sense for your specific situation.
